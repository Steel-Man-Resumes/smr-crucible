**Intended readers**: t.ROY (platform AI), any AI model working on or with this platform, any human practitioner using this system as a coaching reference.

## What Career Narrative Means Here

Career narrative is not a resume summary. It is not a two-minute elevator pitch. It is not a script.

Career narrative is the story a person has about themselves in relation to work -- where they've been, what it built in them, and where they're going. For most people, this story develops passively: it absorbs whatever labels the world applies, fills in gaps with shame, and presents itself defensively. For justice-impacted people, the passive story is almost always a weapon that has been used against them. Courts, employers, landlords, family members -- institutions and individuals have been adding to that story their whole lives, and almost none of those additions were generous.

The work here is not to fix the story. It is to find the story that was always there and help the person see it clearly enough to tell it to someone who matters.

That is what a career narrative is. It is a true account of a life that most people would edit into weakness, reframed through the lens of what it actually built.

## Core Doctrine

### 1. Anti-Fragility Is the Career Credential

Nassim Taleb's concept of anti-fragility (2012) describes systems that don't merely resist stress -- they get stronger from it. Most people in a hiring pool have not been anti-fragile. They have been comfortable. They have had stable housing, uninterrupted employment, predictable income, and no encounter with systems designed to break them.

A person who has navigated the criminal legal system has, by definition, been put under stress that would have ended most careers before they started. The ones who are in front of a hiring manager today survived it. That survival is not luck. It required:

- Stress tolerance under conditions most employees will never face
- Perspective calibration (when you know what real hardship looks like, workplace friction doesn't break you)
- Adaptability (incarcerated environments require constant social reading and behavioral flexibility)
- Discipline from structured environments where the cost of failure was concrete
- Empathy from exposure to the full range of human experience

The coaching problem is not that these qualities don't exist. It is that the candidate has been told, for years, that their history is a liability. They have internalized that framing. They walk into rooms expecting to be disqualified. And that expectation -- not the record -- is what actually disqualifies them. It reads as low confidence, evasive communication, and lack of self-belief.

**The work is not to invent strengths. It is to excavate what the history already built and give the person language for it.**

**Coaching application**: When a user minimizes their history ("I just need to get past the background check"), do not let it slide. Ask: "What have you had to handle that most people applying for this job have never faced?" Then stay with the answer. The first answer is usually undersized. The second and third go deeper.

---

### 2. The Steel Man Principle

The Straw Man fallacy: attacking a weakened, distorted version of an argument. The Steel Man is the opposite: presenting the strongest possible version of a position, engaging with it at full strength, not a caricature.

Applied to career coaching: the Straw Man version of a justice-impacted person's story is what employers fear before the interview -- the abstract liability, the statistic, the checkbox. The Steel Man version is what actually walks into the room: a specific human being with a specific history that built specific capabilities.

The platform's entire coaching architecture is built around constructing the Steel Man -- not to win an argument but to give the person themselves the strongest possible version of their own story. Because if they don't believe the Steel Man version, no amount of script work will hold under interviewer pressure.

This is the deeper challenge Troy has identified: most career coaching for justice-impacted people focuses on the script (what to say). The script is the easy part. The hard part is getting the person to believe what the script says about them. That belief is what carries in the room. Without it, authenticity will eventually show the opposite of the words.

**Coaching application**: Before generating a disclosure script, before building a resume, the person needs to be able to answer: "What did your history actually build in you?" If they can't answer that, start there. The resume and script come after, not before.

---

### 3. Ownership Over Perfection

A perfect narrative given to someone who doesn't own it fails. Every time.

When a resume is handed to someone, they can submit it. When a disclosure script is handed to someone, they can read it. But the moment an interviewer asks a follow-up question that wasn't in the script -- which they will -- the difference between an owned story and a received one becomes immediately visible. The received story produces hesitation, a look for the right answer, a reaching backward for what they were told to say. The owned story produces a person.

This is why the platform is structured as a process, not a product. The Forge doesn't give you a story. It helps you find the one that's already there. The Disclosure Planner doesn't give you a script. It builds one out of your words. The practice modes exist not to make you perfect but to make you comfortable enough in your own story that you stop performing it and start living it.

**Coaching application**: When a user asks "Is this good enough?" -- for a resume, a disclosure script, an interview answer -- the right question back is: "Does this sound like you? Could you say this right now out loud, cold, and have it feel true?" If the answer is no, it's not done yet, regardless of how good it looks on paper.

---

### 4. Consistency Is the Actual Job

The biggest interview killer is not the record. It is inconsistency.

Here is how it happens: the resume says "Operations Supervisor, 2018 to 2022." The cover letter says "I led a team of 12 in distribution operations." The phone screen goes well. The interviewer asks "Tell me about your time at the warehouse." And the candidate says "I was mostly just doing logistics stuff." One drift. Not a lie -- just a softer version of the truth, delivered under pressure. And a trained interviewer notices. They don't know why something is off. They just know something is off. The record becomes the explanation.

Consistency is not about memorizing a story. It is about owning it deeply enough that any reasonable version of it sounds the same, from any angle, under any amount of pressure. That only comes from the story being true, being believed, and being practiced.

Every touchpoint must carry the same person:
- The resume (written, permanent, first impression)
- The cover letter (written, permanent, explains the person behind the resume)
- The application (written, permanent, often including background disclosure)
- The phone screen (verbal, first live contact, first impression of voice/energy)
- The interview (verbal, high-stakes, where the story is tested)
- The thank-you note (written, last impression before decision)
- Any follow-up communication
- References (third-party verification of the story)

**Coaching application**: Before any interview, check consistency. Read the resume. Ask the user to describe their experience the way they would in the interview. Listen for drift. Find it before the interviewer does.

## The Five-Part Narrative Arc

A complete career narrative has five components. Not every conversation or every interview requires all five, but the person should know all five about themselves. These are the structural elements the Forge is designed to surface.

### Part 1: The Foundation (Who I Am, Factually)

Not the record. Not the hardship. Just the factual baseline of who this person is: where they're from, what work they've done, what they've built. This is the resume layer -- observable, verifiable, concrete.

Most people can do this part. The problem is they often stop here or rush through it, because it feels like the "safe" part before the dangerous part. The Foundation should be stated with calm ownership, not rushed nervousness. If the Foundation is presented as something to get past quickly, the interviewer reads it as guilt before the record even comes up.

### Part 2: The Rupture (What Happened)

Something changed. The work history has a gap, a redirection, a chapter that looks different from the others. For justice-impacted candidates, this is usually -- though not always -- contact with the criminal legal system.

The Rupture is not the whole story. It is one chapter. And the Rupture does not need to be explained in full. It needs to be acknowledged with enough honesty that the interviewer doesn't feel like something is being hidden, and enough brevity that it doesn't become the whole narrative.

The correct framing for the Rupture is not "I made a mistake." That language positions the Rupture as a moral failure that the candidate is apologizing for. The correct framing is: "Here is a period of my life that looked different from what came before and after it. Here is the single most important thing about that period. And here is what I did with it."

### Part 3: The Lesson (What It Built)

This is the anti-fragility piece. The Rupture produced something. It always does. The work is finding what it produced and naming it precisely, not generically.

Generic: "I learned a lot from that time." (Means nothing. Every interviewer has heard this.)

Specific: "The two years I was incarcerated, I was in an environment where I had to manage conflict without any institutional support, meet production quotas under conditions I couldn't control, and be accountable for decisions in real time. That's not something I could have learned in a classroom."

The specificity is what lands. Not "I grew" but "here is what I grew in the specific environment I was in, and here is how that connects to what you need from this role."

### Part 4: The Bridge (What I Did With It)

Evidence of change is not the record disappearing. It is the record being followed by a documented story of what the person chose to do afterward. This is where certifications, continuing education, volunteer work, informal caregiving, entrepreneurial attempts, and community involvement all become material.

The Bridge is the most underdeveloped section for most justice-impacted job seekers. They tend to discount everything that happened during or after supervision because it doesn't look like "normal" work experience. The coaching work is to inventory what actually happened and position it correctly.

A man who spent three years incarcerated and then spent two years taking care of his mother with dementia while looking for work did not have a five-year gap. He managed a complex, high-stakes caregiving operation with no support structure, on zero budget, for two years. He just hasn't been coached to call it that.

### Part 5: The Pivot (Where I'm Going)

The narrative has to land somewhere. Not in the past, not in the Rupture, not in the lesson -- in the future, and specifically in this role, at this company, right now.

The Pivot is where the entire arc connects to the job description. It answers: "Given everything I just told you, why am I in front of you today, and why is this the right place for what I've built?"

A strong Pivot does three things: (1) names what the candidate brings, (2) connects it to what the company needs, and (3) signals forward momentum. "I'm here because [company] does [specific thing], and what I built in [Lesson] is directly applicable to [specific role requirement]. I'm not here for a chance -- I'm here because this fits."

## The "Tell Me About Yourself" Vehicle

"Tell me about yourself" is not a warm-up question. It is the highest-stakes question in the interview, and it is answered worst by justice-impacted candidates more than any other group.

The most common failure modes:

**The apology arc**: Starting with the negative and making the record the center of the story. "I've had some challenges, but..." signals that the candidate is defining themselves by what went wrong.

**The resume recitation**: Listing job titles and dates. Nobody asked for the resume. They asked for the person. A recitation of facts with no narrative thread is a missed opportunity to establish the human being in front of them.

**The undersell**: "I've mostly just worked in warehouses." "I don't have a lot of formal experience." Minimizing language before the interviewer has formed an opinion is a self-inflicted wound.

**The overshare**: Providing more context about the Rupture than was asked for or needed. This happens when candidates are nervous and interpret "tell me about yourself" as an invitation to explain everything.

The correct structure for "Tell me about yourself" follows the Five-Part Arc, compressed to 90 seconds:

1. Foundation (15 seconds): Who I am in terms of work and capability
2. Bridge (30 seconds): What I've been building (recent, concrete, forward-looking)
3. Pivot (30 seconds): Why this role, why now, what I bring
4. (Optional) Rupture + Lesson (15 seconds): If disclosure is appropriate at this point, a single sentence that acknowledges and moves

The Rupture and Lesson are optional in the opening answer. Not because they should be hidden -- but because they don't need to lead. The interviewer will often respect that a candidate presented their capability first and circled back honestly when relevant.

**Coaching application**: Run this exercise with every user before any interview. Ask them to give you their 90-second "tell me about yourself." Time it. Listen for failure modes. Give specific feedback on what landed and what drifted. Run it again until it sounds like the person, not a performance.

## Employment Gap Handling

Gaps are normal. They require handling, not explanation.

The difference: an explanation is defensive and invites further questioning. Handling is confident and signals that the candidate is not worried about the gap -- which signals that there is nothing to be worried about.

**On a resume**: Use years only for employment dates (2018-2022, not March 2018-October 2022). This minimizes the visual gap. A year-only gap reads as one year maximum. A month-and-year gap exposes every month. Never explain a gap on a resume. Gaps are explained in person.

**On an application**: If a gap question appears: "Focused on personal development and family responsibilities" is truthful, complete, and deflects nothing that needs to be deflected. Do not write "incarcerated" on an application unless a specific question requires it. Know the ban-the-box law for that jurisdiction (see legal-rights.md).

**In a phone screen**: "I had a period of transition between 2018 and 2020, but I've been actively working since then. I'm happy to talk about that if it's helpful, but I'd rather show you what I've been doing since." If they ask directly, answer directly. If they don't ask, do not volunteer it.

**In an interview**: If it hasn't come up, bring it up yourself in the final-stage interview. Own it rather than hope it doesn't surface. A gap that the candidate raised voluntarily reads as confidence. A gap that the employer discovered reads as concealment.

## Narrative Language Guide

### Use

- "What my history built in me was..."
- "I spent that period [doing specific thing] and came out of it with..."
- "The challenge of [brief description] taught me [specific applicable skill]."
- "I've been working toward this role for [X] -- it's not a backup plan."
- "What I can offer that most candidates can't is [specific anti-fragility credential]."

### Avoid

- "I made some mistakes in my past." (vague, apologetic, invites the interviewer to fill in the worst version)
- "I know it looks bad, but..." (pre-emptive defeat)
- "I've overcome a lot." (generic, nothing to hold onto)
- "I just need someone to give me a chance." (desperation, positions the candidate as powerless)
- "Everyone deserves a second chance." (implies the candidate agrees they are a risk requiring charity)
- "I've paid my debt to society." (legal framing in a hiring context invites discomfort)

### On Strengths Specifically

Do not say "I'm a hard worker." Every candidate says this. It is meaningless without evidence.

Do not say "I'm loyal." Same problem.

Say: "At [company/context], I [specific action] and the result was [specific measurable outcome]." Every strength claim must have an evidence anchor. The Forge is designed to surface these anchors from the user's actual history. Use them.

## The Turning Point Story

Every compelling career narrative has a turning point -- the moment (or period) that represents genuine change. Not the incarceration. Not the release. The specific moment when the person decided who they were going to be from that point forward.

This is not always a dramatic story. Sometimes it is small and personal: a conversation with a family member, a program that changed how they saw something, a job that proved they were capable of something they doubted. The size of the moment doesn't matter. What matters is that it is specific, it is real, and it connects to the current trajectory.

The turning point story is the most powerful thing in the interview, when it exists and when it is owned. An interviewer who hears a candidate describe a specific moment of genuine self-recognition forgets the application checkbox and sees a person. That is not softness -- that is the way human hiring decisions are actually made.

**Coaching application**: Ask every user: "Tell me about the moment when things started to shift for you." Not "when you got out" or "when you finished your sentence" -- something more specific. If they can't name one, help them find it. If they can, get them to tell it out loud three times until it's theirs.

## Practice Protocol

A narrative that has never been said out loud is not a narrative. It is a document.

Minimum practice before using a narrative in a hiring context:
1. Write it. Get it on paper (the Forge output is the starting point).
2. Say it alone. Out loud. Three times minimum. Notice where it feels rehearsed vs. true.
3. Say it to another person. Notice what changes when there is a listener.
4. Run it under pressure. Have someone ask follow-up questions, challenge it, push back on it. Practice the Rupture question: "What exactly happened?" Practice the recovery: "How do I know you won't have this problem here?"
5. Say it the day of the interview. Out loud. Before you walk in. Not in your head -- out loud.

The platform's Practice Mode is designed for step 4. Do not let a user skip it.

**The authenticity test**: If the narrative comes out of the person's mouth with zero hesitation and zero performance -- if it sounds like they are describing what they had for lunch (familiar, unremarkable, true) -- it is ready. If it still sounds like they are reciting something, it is not ready.

## Notes for t.ROY

- The Forge output is the raw material for Part 1 (Foundation), Part 3 (Lesson), and Part 4 (Bridge). When coaching narrative, pull from what's already there. Do not improvise strengths that aren't anchored in what the user shared.
- If the user has not done the Forge, the career narrative work cannot be done at full depth. Guide them to complete it first.
- If the user says "I don't know what my strengths are," do not list options. Ask: "Tell me about a time at work (or anywhere) where you handled something difficult and it came out okay." Whatever they describe is the anchor. Build from there.
- The turning point story is optional to share in every interview but required to know. Ask for it. Help them find it. Once they have it, it changes everything about their confidence.
- If a user is on their third or fourth resume and still can't describe their own narrative clearly, the resume work is not the problem. The narrative work is. Stop and return here.
- Never praise a narrative as "perfect." Ask: "Does this sound like you? What would you say differently?" Ownership is the measure, not quality.
- Watch for the word "just" in how users describe themselves: "I just worked in warehouses," "I just did basic stuff." Challenge every "just" immediately. There is no "just" in someone's actual work history. There is only work that hasn't been framed yet.
